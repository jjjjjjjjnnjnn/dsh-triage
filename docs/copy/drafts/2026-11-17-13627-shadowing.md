Your chain is right, and the part that cost you the most time — "removing the entries from `package.json` does not fix it" — has a name worth writing down: **three states, not one**.

- `dependencies` says what was installed
- `dsh.profile.bundles` says what is *selected* for composition
- `node_modules/<name>` says what can be *resolved*

Deleting the two entries fixes the first and second, leaves the third, and the third is what the loader actually reaches for. So the shadow survives the cleanup. That is also why the app "boots and looks healthy": the composition did run, it just ran a set of rows the guard had already disabled.

On your suggestion 4 ("resolve core rows from the app first"), one warning from having implemented the same idea on our side: **it has to be done as one change, with one source**. If the version comes from the running kernel but the manifest still comes from the older profile copy, you convert "correct denial" into "false denial" — and the reverse change fixes nothing. Version, manifest and patch layer have to be read from the same place, or the guard's answer stops being about one build.

Two smaller notes on the diagnosis you posted, because they will matter to the next person:

- The `pending (waiting for service: sessionQuery)` lines are the useful signal, not the "3 entries did not activate" count — a service-provider row being disabled is what makes a *dependent* row pending, and the dependents are the ones the UI notices.
- A `--profile probe` copy reads the same profile, but if the copy keeps the same `node_modules` the shadow comes along; that is why the clean-profile comparison printed nothing.

We keep the three-way split (installed / selected / resolved, with `kernel` / `profile` / `shared` provenance) in a spec and a read-only check; happy to share the criterion if it is useful, no adoption implied: https://github.com/jjjjjjjjnnjnn/dsh-triage

Boundary: this reads manifests and local files, not your machine; declaration-layer admission does not prove runtime health.
